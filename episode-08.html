Bhagavata Purana · Episode 8 · Textual Commentary

You Won the War. Why Isn't It Over?

Justice, Grief, and Responsibility After the Mahabharata War

Watch Episode 8 on YouTube

Central Question What happens when winning the war does not settle the moral problem?

About the Title

You Won the War. Why Isn't It Over? is an interpretive title rather than a phrase found in the Bhagavata Purana.

It refers especially to Yudhishthira's condition at the end of Chapter 8. The war has been won and political control secured, but Yudhishthira is not morally settled by victory. The text presents him as unable to accept a conventional justification that would make the deaths and suffering connected with the war disappear simply because the cause was judged righteous.

The title therefore points to the central ethical tension of the episode: success in achieving an external objective does not necessarily settle responsibility for what was done, suffered, or lost in reaching it.

Abstract

Episode 8 covers Bhagavata Purana 1.7–1.8. The episode begins with Vyasa's composition of the Bhagavata and the transmission of the text to Shuka, completing the narrative movement opened by Vyasa's dissatisfaction in Episodes 6 and 7.

The narrative then returns sharply to the aftermath of the Mahabharata war. Ashvatthama kills Draupadi's sons and is pursued and captured by Arjuna. What follows is not presented as a simple contest between punishment and forgiveness. Draupadi, Bhima, Krishna, Yudhishthira, and Arjuna bring different obligations into the judgment, and Arjuna must act within those competing claims.

Chapter 8 shifts the direction of the moral problem. The threat extends to Uttara and her unborn child, Parikshit. Krishna's protection of the child secures the future of the lineage and also reconnects the story to the king whose final seven days frame the larger Bhagavata.

The chapter then moves through Kunti's prayer into Yudhishthira's unresolved responsibility. The victor has received the kingdom, but victory has not given him a satisfactory moral account of what the war has cost.

Central Question

What happens when winning the war does not settle the moral problem?

The episode approaches that question through two different forms of judgment.

Chapter 7 directs judgment outward, toward a person who has caused grave harm. Chapter 8 turns judgment inward, toward the victorious king who asks what responsibility remains when suffering has occurred in connection with a cause he believed to be justified.

Interpretive note The contrast between outward judgment in Chapter 7 and inward judgment in Chapter 8 is this project's reading of the narrative movement. It is not a formulation stated explicitly by the Bhagavata Purana.

Textual Scope

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: Vyasa Composes the Bhagavata

Chapter 7 initially continues the narrative of Vyasa from Chapters 4–6.

Following Narada's departure, Vyasa undertakes meditation. The text describes him as perceiving the relation between the divine, maya, and the condition of embodied beings. He then composes the Bhagavata and teaches it to Shuka.

This section completes the Bhagavata's account of why further composition was necessary after the dissatisfaction explored in Episode 7.

The narrative then turns from the composition of the book back toward Parikshit and the events following the Mahabharata war.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: Violence After the War

The war may be over, but the narrative does not present the end of battle as the end of violence.

Ashvatthama kills Draupadi's sons while they are asleep. Arjuna pursues him, and Ashvatthama releases the weapon identified in Tagare as the Brahma-shiras.

The text presents Ashvatthama as unable to withdraw the weapon after releasing it. Arjuna responds with a corresponding weapon and then withdraws both threats before capturing Ashvatthama and bringing him back to the Pandava camp.

The episode treats this sequence carefully because later retellings and the Mahabharata itself can organize these events differently. The Episode 8 discussion follows the sequence presented in the Bhagavata Purana rather than silently combining separate versions.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: Draupadi's Argument

Draupadi does not offer a single reason for restraint.

Her argument moves through several obligations. She considers Ashvatthama's relation to Drona, the position of the teacher's family, the suffering of Ashvatthama's mother, and the consequences of extending grief into another household.

Her response should therefore not be reduced to a vague statement that she simply forgives him.

The passage presents a more complicated act of moral reasoning in which memory of her own suffering becomes part of the reason she resists reproducing that suffering elsewhere.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: Bhima's Demand

Bhima reaches a different conclusion.

For him, Ashvatthama's act requires punishment. His argument keeps accountability in the center of the judgment and prevents Draupadi's appeal for restraint from becoming the only ethical claim present in the scene.

The chapter therefore places compassion and accountability in genuine tension rather than presenting one as obviously ethical and the other as obviously unethical.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: Krishna and Competing Obligations

Krishna's instruction to Arjuna intensifies the conflict rather than simplifying it.

The text places before Arjuna two obligations that do not fit neatly together: the protection associated with brahmana status and the demand that grave wrongdoing receive punishment.

Arjuna's eventual action is therefore read in the episode as a judgment under competing obligations. He does not discover a solution in which every principle can be satisfied without remainder.

Justice Without a Single Rule

Chapter 7 presents ethical judgment as something more difficult than identifying one rule and applying it mechanically.

Draupadi sees obligations connected with grief, kinship, the teacher's household, and restraint. Bhima sees an obligation to accountability. Krishna explicitly places conflicting injunctions before Arjuna.

The chapter does not eliminate those conflicts before requiring action.

The episode therefore reads the judgment of Ashvatthama as a case in which responsibility includes discerning among claims that are all morally significant but cannot all be maximized at once.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8: The Future Is Still Under Attack

Chapter 8 begins with rites for those who have died and the continuing grief of the survivors.

The narrative then introduces a new danger. Uttara approaches Krishna because her unborn child is threatened.

The Bhagavata presents Krishna as directly protecting the unborn Parikshit. This is the same Parikshit whose approaching death later creates the seven-day frame within which the Bhagavata is narrated.

The episode therefore treats this moment as more than the rescue of one individual. Within the narrative architecture of the Bhagavata, the survival of Parikshit preserves the future listener through whom the larger teaching will eventually be heard.

Two Weapon Incidents

Textual clarification The Bhagavata's Chapter 7 sequence and Chapter 8 sequence should not be collapsed into one event. In Chapter 7, the weapon released during Ashvatthama's confrontation with Arjuna is withdrawn. Chapter 8 later introduces a separate threat directed toward the unborn heir. The episode follows the Bhagavata's own sequence rather than importing the ordering of another version of the story.

Tagare's notes also record disagreement among commentators over whether Brahma-shiras and Brahmastra should be treated as the same weapon or as distinct designations.

The episode does not need to settle that disagreement. What matters for the narrative analysis is the sequence presented in the Bhagavata itself.

Kunti After Catastrophe

Kunti's prayer follows a life marked, within the narrative, by repeated danger and loss.

One of the best-known verses in the prayer asks for calamities to continue if those calamities bring the sight of Krishna.

The episode does not read this as a general teaching that suffering should be sought or that suffering is intrinsically good.

Within the surrounding prayer, the verse belongs to Kunti's interpretation of dependence, remembrance, and the danger of forgetting the divine when ordinary security returns.

The distinction is important. A statement about how Kunti interprets her own experience should not automatically be converted into a universal instruction that other people should desire suffering.

Yudhishthira and the Problem of Victory

The final movement of Chapter 8 turns from Kunti's prayer to Yudhishthira.

The war is over. Yudhishthira has the kingdom. He has also heard the conventional claim that a protecting king does not incur sin for killing enemies in a righteous war.

The text nevertheless presents him as unconvinced.

His attention remains on the deaths and suffering connected with the victory, especially the suffering borne by families and women whose losses cannot be undone by the justice of the political outcome.

The episode stops before the next chapter provides Bhishma's extended response.

That boundary matters because Episode 8 does not resolve Yudhishthira's problem prematurely.

Yudhishthira has received a kingdom.
He is still looking for an answer.

Argument and Interpretation

Episode 8 reads Chapters 7 and 8 as a sustained examination of responsibility after catastrophic conflict.

Chapter 7 asks how judgment should proceed when the person before us has committed grave harm and when several ethical obligations point in different directions.

Chapter 8 turns toward another form of responsibility. Yudhishthira is not asking how to judge an enemy. He is asking how to understand his own place within the suffering produced by a war fought on behalf of a cause he believed to be justified.

The two problems are related but not identical.

The episode therefore resists a simple lesson such as “forgive everyone” or “justice always requires punishment.” The text gives us Draupadi and Bhima precisely because morally serious people can perceive different obligations in the same situation.

Yudhishthira then prevents political victory from functioning as moral closure.

Competing Obligations

The judgment of Ashvatthama is especially useful because the narrative does not construct the problem around a conflict between obvious good and obvious evil.

Draupadi's restraint has moral force. Bhima's demand for accountability has moral force. Krishna's instruction preserves both claims long enough that Arjuna must respond without pretending the tension has disappeared.

The episode treats this as a problem of ethical discernment rather than rule selection.

The question is not only which principle is correct. It is how one acts when several principles are relevant at the same time and no available action leaves every obligation untouched.

Responsibility After Justified Action

Yudhishthira's crisis raises a different question.

Suppose an action can be justified. Does that justification exhaust the moral meaning of everything that follows from it?

The Bhagavata does not allow Yudhishthira to answer this simply by repeating that the war was righteous.

His distress keeps attention on consequences that remain morally visible even after the political and legal question appears settled.

The episode reads this as a distinction between justification and moral remainder. An action may be defensible without making every consequence emotionally or ethically irrelevant.

Featured Texts

Bhagavata Purana 1.7: The Judgment of Ashvatthama

The central judgment scene places Draupadi's reasons for restraint, Bhima's demand for punishment, Krishna's competing injunctions, and Arjuna's eventual action within a single moral problem.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.25: Kunti's Prayer

Kunti asks that calamities continue if they bring the sight of Krishna.

The episode reads the verse within the surrounding prayer's logic of dependence and remembrance. It is not presented as a general instruction to seek suffering.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.50: Yudhishthira's Refusal of Easy Consolation

The chapter presents the conventional precept that a protecting king does not incur sin by killing enemies in a righteous war.

Yudhishthira remains unconvinced. The verse is important because it shows that a recognized justification does not automatically settle his moral response to the consequences of the war.

Text, Interpretation, and Comparison

What the text states. The Bhagavata narrates Ashvatthama's actions, Arjuna's pursuit and capture of him, Draupadi's appeal for restraint, Bhima's opposing demand, Krishna's instructions, the threat to the unborn Parikshit, Krishna's protection, Kunti's prayer, and Yudhishthira's unresolved distress.

What this episode argues. The episode reads Chapter 7 as a problem of judging another person under competing obligations and Chapter 8 as a movement toward self-judgment and responsibility after victory. That two-part structure is an interpretive formulation used by this project.

Comparative observation. Modern ethical language concerning just war, moral injury, responsibility, restorative justice, or competing duties may help contemporary readers formulate questions raised by these chapters. Such comparisons should not replace the Bhagavata's own categories or be presented as though the ancient text were using modern ethical theories.

Key Terms

Dharma. In these chapters, dharma cannot be reduced to a single rule. Different obligations associated with status, kinship, punishment, compassion, and responsibility come into conflict.

Brahma-shiras. The designation used in Tagare for the weapon released by Ashvatthama in Chapter 7. Commentarial traditions differ on its relation to the designation Brahmastra.

Karuna / compassion. Compassion is especially visible in Draupadi's reasoning, but the episode does not use compassion as a way of erasing accountability. The scene derives much of its force from the coexistence of both claims.

Responsibility. The episode uses responsibility analytically to describe both the judgment of wrongdoing in Chapter 7 and Yudhishthira's concern with his own participation in the consequences of the war in Chapter 8.

Sources and Editorial Method

Episode 8 relies principally on G.V. Tagare because Chapters 7 and 8 are not represented in Edwin F. Bryant's Bhakti Yoga anthology.

The Gita Press English translation is retained as an additional cross-check.

Special care was taken with narrative sequence because the Bhagavata's presentation of the weapon incidents should not be silently merged with the ordering found in other tellings of the Mahabharata material.

The episode also distinguishes textual statements from interpretive claims. The outward-judgment / inward-judgment structure, for example, belongs to this project's interpretation rather than to an explicit formulation in the Sanskrit text.
