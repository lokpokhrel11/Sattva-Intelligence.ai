Bhagavata Purana · Episode 9 · Canto 1, Chapter 9

What Remains When the Past Cannot Be Changed?

Bhishma, Yudhishthira, and Responsibility After War

Watch Episode 9 on YouTube

Central Question What do you do when the past cannot be undone, but responsibility has not ended?

Episode Overview

Chapter 9 begins where Yudhishthira's unresolved moral crisis in the previous chapter leaves him. The war has ended and the kingdom has been won, but Yudhishthira remains troubled by the destruction through which that victory was achieved. He now goes to Bhishma, who remains on the battlefield, seeking instruction on dharma.

Bhishma first responds to the suffering of the Pandavas. He reflects on Time, providence, and the limits of human understanding. Yet his response does not remain with the problem of explaining the past. He turns Yudhishthira toward those who are still living and tells him, as their ruler and protector, to care for subjects who have been left without protection.

The Bhagavata then compresses Bhishma's extensive teaching on dharma into only a few verses. It mentions duties associated with different conditions of life, attachment and non-attachment, charity, kingship, liberation, what Tagare translates as the “Bhagavata religion,” and the four aims of human life. The chapter does not reproduce the much longer teachings associated with Bhishma in the Mahabharata.

The final movement turns toward Bhishma himself. As death approaches, he concentrates on Krishna. His recollection repeatedly returns to Krishna as he appeared during the war: as Arjuna's charioteer, amid the dust of the battlefield, positioned between the armies, and moving toward Bhishma during combat. Bhishma's final meditation therefore remains connected to the world of action through which he has lived.

After Bhishma's death and funeral rites, Yudhishthira returns to Hastinapura. The chapter closes with him ruling the ancestral kingdom according to dharma.

Interpretive note This episode reads Chapter 9 as moving from the impossibility of changing or fully explaining the past toward the responsibilities that remain in the present. Bhishma does not simply provide Yudhishthira with an explanation that makes the consequences of war disappear. His instruction redirects attention toward protection, rule, and the people who now depend on Yudhishthira. This is an interpretive account of the chapter's narrative movement, not a formulation stated by the Bhagavata Purana itself.

Featured Texts

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.14–17. Bhishma reflects on the power of Time and the difficulty of understanding what has happened to the Pandavas. The movement culminates in verse 17, where he turns from explanation toward Yudhishthira's present obligation as ruler: the protection of subjects who have been left without protection. Translation: G.V. Tagare.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.25–29. Yudhishthira asks Bhishma about different forms of dharma. The text summarizes a broad field of instruction, including duties associated with different conditions of life, attachment and non-attachment, charity, kingship, liberation, what Tagare translates as the “Bhagavata religion,” and the four aims of human life. The teaching is compressed rather than reproduced at length. Translation: G.V. Tagare.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.34–39. Bhishma's final meditation recalls Krishna through scenes from the battlefield. Krishna appears amid the conditions of battle, as Arjuna's charioteer and as the figure Bhishma encountered during combat. By verse 39, Bhishma recalls Krishna holding the reins of Arjuna's horses and carrying the whip. Translation: G.V. Tagare.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.43–49. Bhishma fixes his faculties on Krishna and dies. Yudhishthira performs the funeral rites, the assembled sages depart, and Yudhishthira returns with Krishna to Hastinapura. The chapter concludes with Yudhishthira ruling the ancestral kingdom according to dharma. Translation: G.V. Tagare.

The Past and Present Responsibility

One of the movements in this chapter occurs between Bhishma's explanation of suffering and his instruction to Yudhishthira. Bhishma speaks of Time and providence, and he also acknowledges the difficulty of understanding Krishna's purposes. But the text does not leave Yudhishthira only with an account of why events may have occurred.

Bhishma turns to the condition of the kingdom. People have been left without protection. Yudhishthira is now their ruler.

The distinction is useful for reading the chapter. A person may remain unable to explain the past completely while still having obligations in the present. The Bhagavata does not present those two questions as identical.

Why Bhishma's Final Meditation Returns to the Battlefield

Bhishma's final meditation is not narrated only through images of withdrawal from worldly life. Several of its most vivid scenes come directly from the war.

He remembers Krishna as Arjuna's charioteer. He remembers the dust raised by the horses. He remembers Krishna positioned between the two armies. He remembers Krishna moving toward him in battle.

Within the theological structure of the chapter, these are recollections of Krishna. At the same time, they mean that Bhishma's final concentration does not require the narrative to erase the battlefield from his consciousness. The place of violence, duty, loyalty, and conflict becomes part of the material through which his final meditation is expressed.

This episode therefore does not read Bhishma's death simply as an escape from the world of action. The chapter places his final contemplation in relation to the life and actions that preceded it.

Dharma in Chapter 9

The word dharma carries several functions in this chapter. Yudhishthira approaches Bhishma because he wants to understand different forms of dharma. Bhishma is himself described as someone who knows dharma and understands conduct appropriate to time and place.

Verses 25–28 then widen the field. The chapter refers to duties associated with different circumstances and stages of life, attachment and non-attachment, charity, royal responsibility, liberation, what Tagare translates as the “Bhagavata religion,” and the four aims of human life: dharma, artha, kama, and moksha.

For this reason, translating dharma simply as “religion” or even as “duty” can be too narrow for the chapter as a whole. Tagare's use of “Bhagavata religion” in verse 27 is his translation of that particular expression and should not be taken to mean that dharma everywhere in the chapter is equivalent to the English word “religion.”

Sources and Textual Notes

Chapter 9 is not represented in Bryant's anthology, so this episode relies principally on Tagare's complete translation, with the Sanskrit consulted where particular wording affects the interpretation. See the full Method and Sources page.

Tagare, G.V., trans. The Bhagavata-Purana. Part I. Delhi: Motilal Banarsidass.

The Mahabharata contains a much more extensive body of teaching associated with Bhishma. This episode does not import that material into the Bhagavata Purana. Where broader narrative background is relevant, it is distinguished from what Chapter 9 itself states.

Textual Clarifications and Corrections

No substantive corrections are currently recorded for this episode.

One source distinction is maintained throughout the episode. Chapter 9 itself presents Bhishma lying on the battlefield and physically unable to rise. The familiar bed-of-arrows or bed-of-darts description belongs to the wider narrative tradition and appears in Tagare's accompanying notes, rather than being introduced as the wording of Bhagavata Purana 1.9.1 itself.

Question for Reflection and Discussion

If the past cannot be undone, what kind of responsibility remains in the present? Does Bhishma answer Yudhishthira's moral problem, or does he redirect Yudhishthira toward a different question?
