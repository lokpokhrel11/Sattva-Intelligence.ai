Sanskrit Text & Grammar

Selected grammatical notes on Sanskrit verses discussed in the Sattva Intelligence Bhagavata Purana series. Entries provide padaccheda, morphology, syntax, and literal sense where close attention to the Sanskrit contributes to interpretation.

This is a selective study resource rather than a comprehensive grammatical commentary on the Bhagavata Purana. Verses are included when their Sanskrit wording receives substantive attention in the series or is particularly useful for understanding the passage.

How to Use These Notes

Each entry begins with the Sanskrit text and IAST transliteration, followed by grammatical separation of significant words. The notes identify important forms, compounds, syntactic relationships, and places where close attention to the Sanskrit affects interpretation.

The literal renderings are intentionally close to the Sanskrit. They are working grammatical renderings rather than replacements for published translations. Where a construction admits interpretive difficulty, that difficulty is identified rather than concealed by a smooth English rendering.

These notes complement the Sanskrit Terms guide. The Sanskrit Terms guide follows significant vocabulary across textual contexts. This page asks a different question: how does a Sanskrit word or construction function in a particular verse?

Episode 7: Speech, Literary Form, and Substantive Orientation

These two verses form a deliberate contrast in Narada's instruction to Vyasa. Read together, they distinguish verbal polish from the substantive orientation of speech and composition.

Bhagavata Purana 1.5.10–11

Sanskrit

न यद्वचश्चित्रपदं हरेर्यशो
जगत्पवित्रं प्रगृणीत कर्हिचित् ।
तद्वायसं तीर्थमुशन्ति मानसा
न यत्र हंसा निरमन्त्युशिक्क्षयाः ॥ १० ॥

तद्वाग्विसर्गो जनताघविप्लवो
यस्मिन् प्रतिश्लोकमबद्धवत्यपि ।
नामान्यनन्तस्य यशोऽङ्कितानि यत्
शृण्वन्ति गायन्ति गृणन्ति साधवः ॥ ११ ॥

IAST

na yad vacaś citra-padaṁ harer yaśo
jagat-pavitraṁ pragṛṇīta karhicit |
tad vāyasaṁ tīrtham uśanti mānasā
na yatra haṁsā niramanty uśik-kṣayāḥ || 10 ||

tad-vāg-visargo janatāgha-viplavo
yasmin prati-ślokam abaddhavaty api |
nāmāny anantasya yaśo-'ṅkitāni yat
śṛṇvanti gāyanti gṛṇanti sādhavaḥ || 11 ||

Selected Grammar

hareḥ Genitive singular masculine of Hari
yaśaḥ Neuter singular noun glory; renown
jagat-pavitram Compound purifying the world
pragṛṇīta Verbal form would proclaim or celebrate
vāyasam Adjectival form from vāyasa pertaining to crows
tīrtham Neuter singular noun resort; gathering place; literally a crossing place
mānasāḥ Nominative plural masculine the thoughtful or discerning
haṁsāḥ Nominative plural masculine swans
niramanti Third person plural verb delight; take pleasure
vāg-visargaḥ Compound, nominative singular masculine production or creation of speech
janatāgha-viplavaḥ Compound, nominative singular masculine that which overturns or disrupts wrongdoing among people
prati-ślokam Adverbial expression in each verse; verse by verse
abaddhavati api Locative construction with api even when imperfectly or irregularly composed
nāmāni Nominative/accusative plural neuter names
anantasya Genitive singular masculine of Ananta; of the Unlimited
yaśo-'ṅkitāni Compound, plural neuter marked by or bearing praise/glory
śṛṇvanti Third person plural they hear
gāyanti Third person plural they sing
gṛṇanti Third person plural they utter; praise
sādhavaḥ Nominative plural masculine good or virtuous persons

The Contrast Across the Two Verses

Verse 10 describes the criticized speech as citra-padam. The problem is therefore not simply that the language lacks literary sophistication. The wording may be ornate or striking while still failing to express what Narada regards as substantively significant.

The crow and swan imagery follows this statement. Such speech is called vāyasaṁ tīrtham, a resort associated with crows, and the verse adds na yatra haṁsā niramanti, that the swans do not delight there.

Verse 11 reverses the formal expectation. The contrasting production of speech is described with abaddhavaty api: even where the composition is not perfectly formed or conventionally polished, the virtuous hear it, sing it, and utter it when its content bears the names and praise of Ananta.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

Read together, 1.5.10 and 1.5.11 do not map literary value simply onto polished versus unpolished language. Verse 10 explicitly permits the criticized speech to be citra-pada, verbally ornate, while verse 11 permits the contrasting composition to be abaddha, imperfectly or irregularly composed.

The contrast therefore turns principally on the orientation and substantive content of speech rather than formal polish alone. The crow and swan imagery belongs within that larger argument and should not be detached from it as a general classification of people.

Interpretive Guardrail

The verses make this argument within Narada's theological instruction to Vyasa. Extending the passage into a general theory of communication requires a further interpretive step. The grammatical observation itself is narrower: elaborate form is not sufficient, and imperfect form is not necessarily disqualifying.

Episode 8: Action, Calamity, and Moral Responsibility

These passages examine three distinct problems in Canto 1: action undertaken without knowledge of how to reverse it, Kunti's prayer concerning calamity and seeing Krishna, and Yudhishthira's refusal to treat a general rule about righteous war as sufficient to resolve his own moral responsibility.

Bhagavata Purana 1.7.19–20

Sanskrit

यदाशरणमात्मानमैक्षत श्रान्तवाजिनम् ।
अस्त्रं ब्रह्मशिरो मेने आत्मत्राणं द्विजात्मजः ॥ १९ ॥

अथोपस्पृश्य सलिलं सन्दधे तत्समाहितः ।
अजानन्नपि संहारं प्राणकृच्छ्र उपस्थिते ॥ २० ॥

IAST

yadāśaraṇam ātmānam aikṣata śrānta-vājinam |
astraṁ brahma-śiro mene ātma-trāṇaṁ dvijātmajaḥ || 19 ||

athopaspṛśya salilaṁ sandadhe tat samāhitaḥ |
ajānann api saṁhāraṁ prāṇa-kṛcchra upasthite || 20 ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
aśaraṇam Accusative singular masculine, agreeing with ātmānam without refuge or protection
ātmānam Accusative singular masculine himself
aikṣata Finite verb from √īkṣ he saw; perceived
ātma-trāṇam Compound, accusative singular neuter means of protecting himself
upaspṛśya Absolutive having touched
salilam Accusative singular neuter water
sandadhe Finite verbal form, ātmanepada he applied or deployed
samāhitaḥ Nominative singular masculine concentrated; composed
ajānan Present active participle, nominative singular masculine not knowing
api Indeclinable even; although
saṁhāram Accusative singular masculine withdrawal; retraction
prāṇa-kṛcchre Locative singular when life was in danger
upasthite Locative singular participle having arisen; being present

The Central Construction

The crucial expression is ajānan api saṁhāram: “although not knowing the withdrawal.” The participle ajānan describes the same agent who performs the action expressed by sandadhe.

Verse 19 supplies the decision context. Ashvatthama perceives himself as aśaraṇam, without another refuge, and regards the action as ātma-trāṇam, a means of protecting himself. Verse 20 then states that he proceeds despite not knowing how the action is to be withdrawn.

Cross-reference within the Chapter

The distinction is reinforced later in 1.7.27, where Krishna states that Ashvatthama does not know the withdrawal (naivāsau veda saṁhāram). The narrative subsequently contrasts this incapacity with Arjuna's response to the wider danger.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The passage distinguishes the capacity to initiate an action from the capacity to withdraw it. That distinction arises directly from the narrative and grammar. It does not require treating the ancient passage as a theory of modern technology.

For contemporary comparison, the narrower point is more defensible: an agent may possess sufficient capacity to initiate a consequential process without possessing corresponding capacity to reverse it.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.25

Sanskrit

विपदः सन्तु ताः शश्वत्तत्र तत्र जगद्गुरो ।
भवतो दर्शनं यत्स्यादपुनर्भवदर्शनम् ॥ २५ ॥

IAST

vipadaḥ santu tāḥ śaśvat tatra tatra jagad-guro |
bhavato darśanaṁ yat syād apunar-bhava-darśanam || 25 ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
vipadaḥ Nominative plural feminine calamities; adversities
santu Third person plural imperative of √as let them be
tāḥ Nominative plural feminine those
śaśvat Indeclinable continually; repeatedly
tatra tatra Repeated adverbial expression there and there; wherever they occur
jagad-guro Vocative singular masculine O teacher of the world
bhavataḥ Genitive singular of you; your
darśanam Nominative/accusative singular neuter seeing; sight; encounter
yat Relative pronoun which; that which
syāt Optative, third person singular of √as might be; would be
apunar-bhava-darśanam Compound, nominative/accusative singular neuter not seeing renewed becoming/birth again

The Structure of the Prayer

The opening vipadaḥ santu is striking: “let there be calamities.” But the grammatical thought does not end there. The following lines provide the relation that gives the statement its force:

bhavato darśanaṁ yat syād

“through which there might be seeing of you,” followed by apunar-bhava-darśanam.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The verse should therefore not be reduced to the statement “Kunti wants suffering.” The requested recurrence of vipad is grammatically connected with darśana, seeing Krishna, and that seeing is in turn connected with apunar-bhava.

The object of the prayer is consequently not adversity considered in isolation. The complete syntax connects adversity, seeing, and release from renewed becoming.

Bhagavata Purana 1.8.50

Sanskrit

नैनो राज्ञः प्रजाभर्तुर्धर्मयुद्धे वधो द्विषाम् ।
इति मे न तु बोधाय कल्पते शासनं वचः ॥ ५० ॥

IAST

naino rājñaḥ prajā-bhartur dharma-yuddhe vadho dviṣām |
iti me na tu bodhāya kalpate śāsanaṁ vacaḥ || 50 ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
na enaḥ Negation + neuter noun not wrongdoing; not sin
rājñaḥ Genitive singular masculine of a king
prajā-bhartuḥ Genitive singular masculine compound of the protector/supporter of the subjects
dharma-yuddhe Locative singular compound in a dharmic or righteous battle
vadhaḥ Nominative singular masculine killing
dviṣām Genitive plural of enemies
me Genitive/dative enclitic pronoun for me; to me
bodhāya Dative singular masculine for understanding; for conviction
kalpate Finite verb, third person singular, ātmanepada serves; is adequate; is suitable
śāsanam Neuter noun instruction; injunction
vacaḥ Neuter noun statement; word

The Grammatical Contrast

The first half states a general proposition concerning a king who protects subjects and the killing of enemies in dharma-yuddha.

Yudhishthira then responds:

iti me na tu bodhāya kalpate śāsanaṁ vacaḥ

The instructional statement does not, for him, suffice for bodha. The dative bodhāya identifies the end or purpose for which the statement proves inadequate.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The verse distinguishes possession of a general justificatory rule from Yudhishthira's own moral understanding of his action. He is not presented as unaware of the rule. Rather, the rule itself does not resolve the problem for him.

This makes the passage useful for distinguishing formal justification from continuing moral judgment without claiming that the verse rejects dharma-yuddha as a category.

Episode 9: Bhishma, Dharma, and the Final Direction of Attention

The following entries examine selected passages from Canto 1, Chapter 9. Together they move from Bhishma's knowledge of dharma, through responsibility and differentiated forms of dharma, to the withdrawal of speech and the direction of his mind toward Krishna.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.9

Key Sanskrit

पूजयामास धर्मज्ञो देशकालविभागवित्

pūjayām āsa dharma-jño deśa-kāla-vibhāga-vit

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
pūjayām āsa Periphrastic perfect he honored
dharma-jñaḥ Compound, nominative singular masculine one who knows dharma
deśa Noun place
kāla Noun time
vibhāga-vit Compound, nominative singular masculine one who knows distinctions or divisions

Why the Sanskrit Matters

Bhishma is characterized not simply as dharma-jñaḥ, “one who knows dharma,” but also as deśa-kāla-vibhāga-vit, one who knows distinctions of place and time.

The wording supports a restrained interpretive point: knowledge of dharma is presented here together with sensitivity to distinctions of circumstance. It does not require the broader claim that dharma is merely relative.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.10

Key Sanskrit

कृष्णं च तत्प्रभावज्ञ आसीनं जगदीश्वरम् ।
हृदिस्थं पूजयामास माययोपात्तविग्रहम् ॥

kṛṣṇaṁ ca tat-prabhāva-jña āsīnaṁ jagad-īśvaram |
hṛdi-sthaṁ pūjayām āsa māyayopātta-vigraham ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
kṛṣṇam Accusative singular masculine Krishna
tat-prabhāva-jñaḥ Compound, nominative singular masculine knowing his power or greatness
āsīnam Present participle, accusative singular masculine seated
jagad-īśvaram Compound, accusative singular masculine Lord of the world
hṛdi-stham Compound, accusative singular masculine situated in the heart
māyayā Instrumental singular feminine by / through māyā
upātta-vigraham Compound, accusative singular masculine having assumed or taken a form

Grammatical Relationship

The accusative forms āsīnam, jagad-īśvaram, hṛdi-stham, and upātta-vigraham all characterize kṛṣṇam. The grammar therefore holds together Krishna's visible presence, his identification as Lord of the world, his presence in the heart, and the statement concerning form and māyā.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The verse should not be reduced to the isolated occurrence of māyā. Its meaning emerges from the whole grammatical construction. Interpretation of precisely what māyayopātta-vigraham implies should therefore be distinguished from the basic grammatical observation.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.17

Sanskrit

तस्मादिदं दैवतन्त्रं व्यवस्य भरतर्षभ ।
तस्यानुविहितोऽनाथा नाथ पाहि प्रजाः प्रभो ॥

IAST

tasmād idaṁ daiva-tantraṁ vyavasya bharatarṣabha |
tasyānuvihito ’nāthā nātha pāhi prajāḥ prabho ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
tasmāt Ablative used adverbially therefore
daiva-tantram Compound, accusative singular neuter dependent upon / governed by divine ordering
vyavasya Absolutive having determined or ascertained
bharatarṣabha Vocative singular O best of the Bharatas
anāthāḥ Nominative plural without a protector
nātha Vocative singular O protector / lord
pāhi Imperative, second person singular, √pā protect
prajāḥ Accusative plural feminine subjects; people under one's protection

Syntax

The decisive grammatical movement comes at pāhi prajāḥ: “protect the subjects.” The imperative pāhi turns the passage from recognition of a larger ordering toward a concrete obligation addressed to Yudhishthira.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

Recognition that events are embedded in forces beyond individual control does not end the sentence in passivity. Bhishma moves to an imperative. Yudhishthira still has subjects requiring protection. The grammar therefore provides a textual basis for reading the passage as a movement from recognition of limits toward remaining responsibility.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.26

पुरुषस्वभावविहितान् यथावर्णं यथाश्रमम् ।

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
puruṣa-svabhāva-vihitān Compound, accusative plural masculine prescribed or established according to human nature
yathā-varṇam Adverbial compound according to varṇa

The ending -bhyām in upādhibhyām is dual. The construction therefore explicitly pairs vairāgya and rāga.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.27–28

Key Sanskrit Vocabulary

Verse 27 organizes Bhishma's teaching through a sequence of compounds:

dāna-dharmān · rāja-dharmān · mokṣa-dharmān · strī-dharmān · bhagavad-dharmān

Verse 28 then explicitly brings together:

dharma · artha · kāma · mokṣa

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
dāna-dharmān Accusative plural compound dharmas concerning giving
rāja-dharmān Accusative plural compound dharmas of kingship
mokṣa-dharmān Accusative plural compound dharmas concerning liberation
strī-dharmān Accusative plural compound dharmas associated with women
bhagavad-dharmān Accusative plural compound dharmas related to Bhagavān
vibhāgaśaḥ Adverb according to divisions or categories
dharma-artha-kāma-mokṣān Coordinated compound, accusative plural dharma, material well-being, desire, and liberation
sahopāyān Accusative plural together with their means
tattva-vit Nominative singular masculine one who knows reality / truth

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The passage does not present dharma as an undifferentiated category. Verse 27 grammatically enumerates different domains of dharma. Verse 28 then places dharma within the fourfold sequence of dharma, artha, kāma, and mokṣa, together with their means.

The grammar therefore reinforces the chapter's concern with differentiation rather than a single abstract rule applied without attention to domain or purpose.

Bhagavata Purana 1.9.32

Sanskrit

इति मतिरुपकल्पिता वितृष्णा
भगवति सात्वतपुङ्गवे विभूम्नि ।

bhagavati sātvata-puṅgave vibhūmni |

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
matiḥ Nominative singular feminine mind; thought; intention
upakalpitā Past passive participle, nominative singular feminine directed; placed; dedicated
vitṛṣṇā Nominative singular feminine free from thirst or craving
bhagavati Locative singular in / toward Bhagavān
sātvata-puṅgave Locative singular compound in the foremost of the Sātvatas

Vitṛṣṇā and Vairāgya

The text here uses vitṛṣṇā. It does not use vairāgya. Earlier in the same chapter, 1.9.26 explicitly uses vairāgya in the compound vairāgya-rāgopādhibhyām.

The terms occupy overlapping conceptual territory but are not lexically identical and do not perform the same grammatical function in these passages. In 1.9.26, vairāgya is paired with rāga. Here vitṛṣṇā characterizes matiḥ, Bhishma's mind or intention.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

Freedom from tṛṣṇā in this verse does not amount to an absence of attention. The same grammatical construction describes the mati as directed toward Bhagavān. The verse therefore places freedom from craving alongside concentrated orientation, rather than simple indifference.

Bhagavata Purana 1.10.22

Sanskrit

स एव भूयो निजवीर्यचोदितां
स्वजीवमायां प्रकृतिं सिसृक्षतीम् ।
अनामरूपात्मनि रूपनामनी

IAST

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
saḥ Pronoun, nominative singular masculine he; that one
bhūyaḥ Indeclinable again
nija-vīrya-coditām Accusative singular feminine impelled by his own power
prakṛtim Accusative singular feminine prakṛti; nature
sisṛkṣatīm Desiderative participle, accusative singular feminine desiring or preparing to create

vidhitsamānaḥ Desiderative participle, nominative singular masculine intending to establish or assign

Grammatical Relationships

Several feminine accusative singular forms belong with prakṛtim. The morphology therefore groups these expressions around prakṛti rather than presenting them as unrelated concepts.

The form sisṛkṣatīm describes prakṛti, whereas vidhitsamānaḥ is masculine nominative singular and refers to the subject. The grammar therefore distinguishes their functions.

The form rūpa-nāmanī is dual: “form and name.” The pairing is grammatically explicit.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

Verse 1.10.22 begins with sa eva, “that very same one,” linking its subject to the Puruṣa of 1.10.21. It then introduces prakṛtim as grammatically distinct from that subject.

The two verses should therefore be read together before importing the complete technical structure of another philosophical system.

Bhagavata Purana 1.12.30

Sanskrit

स एष लोके विख्यातः परीक्षिदिति यत्प्रभुः ।
पूर्वं दृष्टमनुध्यायन् परीक्षेत नरेष्विह ॥ ३० ॥

IAST

sa eṣa loke vikhyātaḥ parīkṣid iti yat prabhuḥ |
pūrvaṁ dṛṣṭam anudhyāyan parīkṣeta nareṣv iha || 30 ||

Selected Grammar

Form Analysis Basic sense here
loke Locative singular in the world
vikhyātaḥ Past passive participle, nominative singular masculine known; renowned
parīkṣit Proper name Parikshit
pūrvam Adverbial use previously; before
dṛṣṭam Past passive participle, accusative singular neuter what had been seen
anudhyāyan Present active participle, nominative singular masculine contemplating; reflecting upon
parīkṣeta pari + √īkṣ, optative, third person singular, ātmanepada he would / might examine
nareṣu Locative plural among people

Name and Verbal Association

The verse places the proper name Parīkṣit close to the verbal form parīkṣeta, “he would examine.” This creates a textual association between the prince's name and examination within the narrative.

This is best described as the Bhagavata's narrative and linguistic association. The verse by itself should not be used to establish a historical etymology of the proper name.

Why the Sanskrit Matters

The construction pūrvaṁ dṛṣṭam anudhyāyan establishes something previously seen as the object of continuing contemplation, while parīkṣeta describes subsequent examination among people.

The sequence is therefore more specific than generalized curiosity: previous perception, continuing contemplation, and examination are grammatically connected.
