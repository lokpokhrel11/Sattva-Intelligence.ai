Which One Is Running You?

Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapters 18–19

Watch Episode 2 on YouTube

Central Question How do sattva, rajas, and tamas shape the way a person perceives, responds, and acts?

About the Title

Which One Is Running You? is an interpretive title rather than a phrase taken directly from the Bhagavata Purana. It refers to the episode's use of the three guṇas, sattva, rajas, and tamas, as a framework for examining changing states of attention, motivation, and action.

The wording should not be taken to suggest that the guṇas are fixed personality types. The episode treats them as shifting qualities or tendencies whose relative dominance can change across situations.

Abstract

Episode 2 continues directly from the narrative situation established in Episode 1. The two episodes deliberately overlap in Chapter 18: Episode 1 carries the curse pronounced against King Parikshit, while Episode 2 begins from the king's response and moves into Chapter 19, where he turns away from kingship, settles on the bank of the Ganga, encounters the gathering of sages, and receives Shuka.

Alongside this narrative movement, the episode introduces sattva, rajas, and tamas as a philosophical framework for examining perception, motivation, and action. Rather than treating sattva as simple moral goodness or rajas and tamas as simple moral evil, the episode asks what each quality does when it becomes dominant, and how a person might learn to recognize those shifts without turning them into permanent identities.

Central Question

How do sattva, rajas, and tamas shape the way a person perceives, responds, and acts?

The episode approaches this question through Parikshit's changing circumstances. The same king who reacts impulsively in Chapter 18 later receives the consequences of that action, relinquishes political authority, and turns toward sustained inquiry. The guṇa framework provides one comparative way of thinking about this change in orientation.

Textual Scope

Bhagavata Purana 1.18: The Consequence of the Curse

Episode 1 follows the events that culminate in Sringi's curse. Episode 2 deliberately returns to Chapter 18 in order to carry the other side of that event: Parikshit's response once the consequence of his earlier action becomes known.

The overlap between Episodes 1 and 2 is therefore intentional. Episode 1 emphasizes the event and the judgment surrounding the curse. Episode 2 begins from what the king does after the situation can no longer be reversed.

The Chapter 18 overlap between Episodes 1 and 2 is deliberate and has been retained as part of the project's episode architecture.

Bhagavata Purana 1.19: The Arrival of Shuka

Parikshit withdraws from ordinary royal life and takes his place beside the Ganga. Sages gather around him, creating the setting in which the central question of his remaining life can be asked.

Shuka then arrives. The narrative places an emperor before a young renunciant who possesses no political office, institutional position, or visible material status. Parikshit receives him with respect and turns toward inquiry.

This reversal is central to the frame narrative. Political power no longer determines who speaks with authority. The king has the question, while the person who appears to own almost nothing becomes the teacher.

Argument and Interpretation

Episode 2 reads Parikshit's situation through a question different from the one emphasized in Episode 1. Episode 1 asks what becomes worth knowing when time is limited. Episode 2 asks what kind of mind is capable of responding to that limit.

This is where the three-guṇa framework enters the discussion. The episode does not treat sattva, rajas, and tamas as three permanent kinds of people. It uses them as a way of thinking about changing configurations of clarity, activity, restlessness, inertia, obscuration, and attention.

Parikshit's own narrative makes such a framework useful. In Chapter 18, a moment of frustration produces an action with consequences disproportionate to its immediate cause. By Chapter 19, the king's orientation has changed. He no longer directs his energy toward preserving status or avoiding the consequence. He turns toward inquiry.

The episode does not claim that the text explicitly labels each stage of Parikshit's behavior by a particular guṇa. That application belongs to the episode's interpretive framework. The distinction matters because the project separates what the Bhagavata states from the philosophical comparisons used to think through what it states.

The Three Gunas

Sattva

Sattva is associated with clarity, luminosity, intelligibility, and relative balance. In the episode, it is discussed as a condition in which perception can become less clouded by agitation or inertia.

Sattva should not be reduced to the English word “goodness.” Such a translation can make a dynamic quality sound like a fixed moral identity. The episode instead emphasizes what sattva allows: greater clarity, steadiness, and capacity to discern.

Rajas

Rajas is associated with activity, movement, desire, striving, and restlessness. It is not treated as inherently evil. Action, effort, ambition, and transformation all require movement.

The problem arises when movement becomes self-perpetuating, when the demand to act, acquire, respond, or control makes stillness and clear judgment difficult.

Tamas

Tamas is associated with inertia, heaviness, obscuration, resistance, and diminished clarity. Like rajas, it is not presented merely as a villain. Rest, stability, and material persistence all involve forms of inertia.

The ethical and cognitive problem appears when obscuration becomes dominant and a person becomes less able or willing to perceive what a situation requires.

Why Sattva Is Not a Personality Type

The episode treats the guṇas as changing modes rather than labels to attach permanently to people. A person may display clarity in one situation, restlessness in another, and inertia in a third.

This distinction also prevents the framework from becoming a hierarchy of personal worth. To describe a state as sattvic, rajasic, or tamasic is not the same as declaring that one person is intrinsically superior to another.

The Problem of Chasing Sattva

The episode also considers a paradox within the pursuit of clarity. If sattva itself becomes an object of anxious acquisition, the attempt to become calm or pure can become another form of striving.

A person may therefore attempt to overcome rajas through an increasingly rajasic effort to manufacture sattva. The episode uses this possibility to distinguish between observing a condition of mind and turning that condition into another achievement project.

Featured Texts

Bhagavata Purana 1.18–19. These chapters provide the primary narrative basis for the episode: Parikshit's response to the curse, his withdrawal from kingship, the gathering on the bank of the Ganga, and the arrival of Shuka.

Question for Reflection and Discussion

The guṇas offer a way of observing changing states without immediately turning them into fixed identities.

When you look at your own experience, what usually tells you that sattva, rajas, or tamas has become dominant? Is it your attention, your speech, your energy, your decisions, or something else?
