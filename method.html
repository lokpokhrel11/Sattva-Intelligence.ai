Research practice

Method and Sources

The kind of evidence required should match the kind of claim being made. Translation, interpretation, comparison, and application are related but distinct activities, and they are not silently collapsed into one another.

Sattva Intelligence is a research and public-humanities project devoted to close reading, philosophical interpretation, and interdisciplinary inquiry into classical Indic texts and contemporary questions.

1. Textual Method and Sources

Work begins with an identifiable primary-text passage. For the Bhagavata Purana series, each episode is assigned a defined canto and chapter range; speakers, narrative sequence, and chapter boundaries are treated as part of the evidence. Sanskrit is consulted where a term or construction materially affects the interpretation, not to lend an argument extra authority.

The Bhagavata Purana series works principally with Edwin F. Bryant's Bhakti Yoga (North Point Press, 2017), used where his selective anthology covers the passage; G.V. Tagare's complete translation (Motilal Banarsidass), used for full coverage and independent comparison; and the Gita Press English translation as an additional traditional source. This hierarchy is functional, not doctrinal — priority reflects coverage, not presumed correctness. Where an episode draws on more than one translation, that mixed-source character is disclosed.

Living traditions, such as commentary or the Tattvachintamani, illuminate how a text continues to be read and practiced, but are not treated as evidence for what an earlier passage itself states. These sources inform one another; they are not substituted for one another.

2. Sanskrit and Terminology

Sanskrit terms such as dharma, buddhi, viveka, and bhakti carry different meanings across texts, schools, and commentarial traditions. The relevant question is not “what does this word mean,” but “what does it mean here, in this passage and argument.” A definition suited to the Yoga Sutras should not be assumed to transfer automatically to the Gita or the Bhagavata Purana.

Translations are treated as interpretive witnesses, not transparent substitutes for the Sanskrit, and are compared where differences materially affect an argument, following a principle of proportional scrutiny rather than exhaustive comparison everywhere. Etymology is evidence about a word's formation, not its use; an attractive derivation does not by itself establish meaning.

Scholarly pages may use standard transliteration with diacritics where precision requires it. Spoken presentation in the video series uses simplified Roman spelling for ease of pronunciation and oral clarity. This is a presentation distinction, not a difference in textual standard.

3. Levels of Claim

Four levels of claim are distinguished, to make clear what kind of evidence would justify each. A textual claim states what a source says, without asserting that the position described is historically or philosophically correct. An interpretive claim proposes what a passage means or how a concept functions within its own setting. A comparative claim relates a text to another tradition or discipline — resemblance justifies investigation, not identity or influence. A contemporary application brings classical material into conversation with a present problem; a classical concept can inform a discussion of, say, artificial intelligence without the source itself containing such a theory.

Movement between these levels is expected. The requirement is that the movement stay visible.

4. Comparative Method

Sattva Intelligence brings Indic sources into conversation with contemporary philosophy, psychology, and other fields where the comparison is warranted. Its governing principle: comparison is not validation. A resemblance does not establish identity, historical influence, or that a classical tradition anticipated a modern finding, nor does modern science confer authority backward onto a classical claim. Modern scholarship is an interlocutor, not a tribunal, and the reverse is also avoided: antiquity does not lend authority to a modern theory merely because the two resemble each other.

5. Difference Within Indic Traditions

“Indic thought” names a broad category, not one system. Vedanta, Samkhya-Yoga, Nyaya, Buddhist traditions, and others disagree substantively on selfhood, knowledge, and liberation, and shared vocabulary does not erase that disagreement. Where traditions agree, the grounds of agreement are named; where they differ, the difference stays visible. This becomes increasingly important as the project expands beyond the Bhagavata Purana.

6. Verification and Corrections

The relevant question for any citation is not whether one exists, but whether it supports the claim at the level the claim is being made — a translation shows how a translator rendered a passage, not the historical development of a doctrine. Stable references (canto, chapter, verse) are used where possible, since pagination varies by edition. Where a source leaves something unstated, the project resists filling the gap unless the inference is identified as such, and where evidence is genuinely uncertain, that uncertainty is stated rather than resolved by confident prose.

Substantive errors, affecting attribution, translation, sequence, or interpretation, are corrected publicly when they materially affect the record, maintained in the Corrections Log. A distinction is kept between correction, addressing inaccuracy, and revision, recording a genuinely developed interpretation; not every change of mind is treated as an earlier error.

7. Scope and Limitations

This is not a critical edition and does not undertake manuscript collation or replace specialist philological scholarship, nor does it claim a single authoritative reading of any tradition it studies; interpretations here should be assessed on their evidence and reasoning. Cosmological, chronological, and theological material is attributed to the text itself (“the Bhagavata describes,” “within the text's account”) rather than converted into claims of modern history or science. Public accessibility does not remove the obligation of accuracy: videos simplify for oral presentation, while these pages carry the fuller citations, qualifications, and corrections.

Methodological transparency does not mean the project has no point of view. It means distinguishing the evidence from the argument built on it.
