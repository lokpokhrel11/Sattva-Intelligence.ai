Bhagavata Purana · Episode 11

Arjuna Still Had His Bow. So What Changed?

Dhṛtarāṣṭra's Departure, Arjuna's Return, and the Rise of Parīkṣit

Canto 1, Chapters 13–15

Watch Episode 11 Return to Episode Index

Episode Overview

Episode 11 follows the final movement of the Pāṇḍavas' story in the First Canto. Vidura returns to Hastinapura and confronts Dhṛtarāṣṭra with a difficult reality: the political and familial world to which he remains attached has already disappeared.

After Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Gāndhārī leave the palace, Yudhiṣṭhira faces a different problem. His concern for them is genuine, but Nārada asks him to recognize that care does not give one person permanent control over another person's path.

The narrative then turns to Arjuna. After seven months away, he returns from Dvārakā visibly distressed. Kṛṣṇa has departed, and an experience of failure forces Arjuna to reconsider capacities he had previously understood as his own. The bow remains, the warrior remains, but Arjuna no longer understands his former power in the same way.

The episode closes with Yudhiṣṭhira transferring royal authority to Parīkṣit before the Pāṇḍavas themselves depart. Chapter 15 therefore brings the narrative directly to Chapter 16, the point at which this series originally began.

Text Covered

Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapters 13–15.

Chapter 13 follows Vidura's return, his confrontation with Dhṛtarāṣṭra, and the departure of Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Gāndhārī. Chapter 14 turns to Yudhiṣṭhira's unease during Arjuna's prolonged absence and the signs of disorder he perceives around him. Chapter 15 gives Arjuna's account of Kṛṣṇa's departure and concludes with Yudhiṣṭhira's transfer of authority to Parīkṣit and the departure of the Pāṇḍavas.

Primary Source

The principal English source for this episode is G. V. Tagare's translation of the Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapters 13–15.

The episode follows the narrative sequence of these chapters closely. Sanskrit wording is treated cautiously, and interpretive claims are distinguished from what the text itself directly states.

Key Textual and Philosophical Notes

1. Vidura's Return and Silence

Vidura returns to the Kuru household after his pilgrimage. During his travels he has learned of the destruction of the Yādavas, but the Bhagavata Purana presents his decision not to tell the Pāṇḍavas as an act of compassion.

His return soon shifts the narrative toward Dhṛtarāṣṭra. The central problem is no longer the war itself but what remains when the conditions that organized a person's former life have already disappeared.

2. Vidura's Confrontation with Dhṛtarāṣṭra

In Bhagavata Purana 1.13.18, Vidura tells Dhṛtarāṣṭra directly to leave. The force of the instruction comes from Dhṛtarāṣṭra's present condition. His sons are dead, his former political authority is gone, and he now lives under the protection of the family that survived the consequences of his sons' actions.

The episode does not treat this passage as a general condemnation of aging or dependence. Vidura's rebuke addresses Dhṛtarāṣṭra's particular history of attachment to dynasty, succession, and political power.

3. Dhṛtarāṣṭra Leaves

Vidura eventually instructs Dhṛtarāṣṭra to leave the household and go north without announcing his departure to the family. Gāndhārī goes with him.

For Dhṛtarāṣṭra, departure requires recognizing that remaining in the palace cannot restore the world he has lost. The narrative therefore moves from recognition to action: he actually leaves the setting that had embodied his former identity.

4. Yudhiṣṭhira and the Limits of Care

When Yudhiṣṭhira discovers that Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Gāndhārī are gone, his first response is concern. He had assumed responsibility for their care after the war and fears what may have happened to them.

Nārada reframes the situation. Human beings come together and separate, and care does not make another person's life permanently controllable. The same departure therefore carries two different meanings. For Dhṛtarāṣṭra, leaving means releasing a life he can no longer continue. For Yudhiṣṭhira, allowing him to leave means recognizing a limit to his own responsibility.

5. Arjuna's Seven-Month Absence

Chapter 14 begins with Arjuna still absent after seven months. He had gone to Dvārakā to see Kṛṣṇa and the Yādava family. The delay causes Yudhiṣṭhira to remember Nārada's earlier indication that Kṛṣṇa would not remain visibly present in the world forever.

The chapter surrounds this uncertainty with signs of disorder. Yudhiṣṭhira does not yet know what has happened. He is attempting to interpret a changed world before receiving the information that will explain it.

6. Reading Arjuna's Return

When Arjuna finally returns, Yudhiṣṭhira first reads his condition rather than receiving an immediate explanation. Arjuna is visibly disturbed, and Yudhiṣṭhira considers several possible causes connected with responsibility, protection, and moral failure.

Only gradually does his questioning return to Kṛṣṇa. The narrative therefore places Yudhiṣṭhira in a position different from Dhṛtarāṣṭra's: Dhṛtarāṣṭra resisted recognizing a change that had already happened, while Yudhiṣṭhira senses change before its cause has been confirmed.

7. What Changed for Arjuna?

Chapter 15 confirms what Yudhiṣṭhira feared: Kṛṣṇa has departed. Arjuna's response is not limited to grief. He begins reconsidering his own past achievements and the relation between his apparent capacities and Kṛṣṇa's presence.

On his return journey, Arjuna had attempted to protect Kṛṣṇa's surviving wives and failed. The experience becomes the immediate setting for his reflection on the power he had once exercised.

8. The Bow Remains — Bhagavata Purana 1.15.21

Bhagavata Purana 1.15.21 is central to the episode. Arjuna reflects on the bow, arrows, chariot, horses, and his own continuing presence. The external instruments associated with his former efficacy remain, yet he no longer understands them as sufficient in themselves.

The episode deliberately avoids reducing Kṛṣṇa to a modern symbol for confidence, friendship, favorable circumstances, or psychological support. Arjuna's reflection is theological within the Bhagavata Purana: the capacities that appeared to belong to him are reconsidered in relation to Kṛṣṇa.

9. Remembering Kṛṣṇa's Teaching

Arjuna's crisis does not end by restoring the conditions that existed before Kṛṣṇa's departure. Instead, the text turns toward recollection. Arjuna remembers Kṛṣṇa's teaching, and his agitation begins to subside.

The distinction is significant. Kṛṣṇa's physical presence is gone, but what Arjuna learned from him has not simply disappeared. The old world is not restored; Arjuna's understanding of it changes.

10. Losing Power and Relinquishing It

The closing movement places Yudhiṣṭhira beside Dhṛtarāṣṭra without making their situations identical. Dhṛtarāṣṭra had already lost the kingdom before leaving the palace. Yudhiṣṭhira still possesses royal authority when he decides to transfer it.

Yudhiṣṭhira installs Parīkṣit at Hastinapura and makes provision for succession before beginning his own departure. Renunciation therefore does not appear here simply as abandoning responsibility. The transfer of responsibility precedes departure.

11. Parīkṣit and the Return to Episode 1

The end of Chapter 15 brings the series to a structural turning point. The Pāṇḍavas leave after transferring authority to the next generation. Chapter 16 then begins with Parīkṣit ruling.

This is the chapter at which the Sattva Intelligence Bhagavata Purana series originally began. Episode 11 therefore completes the narrative movement that leads directly to the opening position of Episode 1.

Interpretive Caution

The episode brings Dhṛtarāṣṭra, Arjuna, and Yudhiṣṭhira into a shared narrative discussion of change, recognition, power, and departure. Their situations should not, however, be collapsed into a single psychological lesson.

Dhṛtarāṣṭra must recognize a world that has already ended. Yudhiṣṭhira first senses a change whose cause has not yet been confirmed. Arjuna discovers that familiar instruments and abilities no longer carry their former efficacy in the absence of Kṛṣṇa. The episode places these experiences in relation while preserving their differences.

Key Sanskrit Terms and Names

Dhṛtarāṣṭra (धृतराष्ट्र) — the former Kuru king whom Vidura confronts in Chapter 13. His departure from the palace begins the episode's examination of attachment, changed circumstances, and relinquishment.

Vidura (विदुर) — Dhṛtarāṣṭra's brother, whose return and direct counsel initiate the movement toward Dhṛtarāṣṭra's departure.

Gāndhārī (गान्धारी) — wife of Dhṛtarāṣṭra, who leaves the royal household with him.

Yudhiṣṭhira (युधिष्ठिर) — the Pāṇḍava king whose concern for Dhṛtarāṣṭra and Gāndhārī develops into a larger question about responsibility, care, and eventually the relinquishment of his own authority.

Nārada (नारद) — the sage who responds to Yudhiṣṭhira's distress after the elders' departure and places separation within a larger account of human existence.

Arjuna (अर्जुन) — the Pāṇḍava warrior whose return after Kṛṣṇa's departure leads him to reconsider the source and meaning of his former power.

Kṛṣṇa (कृष्ण) — Arjuna's friend and the central divine figure whose departure provides the theological context for Arjuna's reflection in Chapter 15.

Parīkṣit (परीक्षित्) — the successor installed at Hastinapura before the Pāṇḍavas depart. Chapter 16 begins with his reign, reconnecting Episode 11 directly with Episode 1.

Connections Across the Series

Episode 11 follows directly from Episode 10, where Parīkṣit's birth and naming established the generation that would follow the Pāṇḍavas. Episode 11 now shows how authority actually passes to him.

The episode also reconnects structurally with Episode 1. The series began at Chapter 16 with Parīkṣit already ruling. Episode 11 reaches the end of Chapter 15 and therefore supplies the immediate narrative transition into that beginning.

Discussion Question

Dhṛtarāṣṭra leaves only after the world that supported his former position has already disappeared, while Yudhiṣṭhira relinquishes authority that he still possesses. What does the contrast between these two departures add to the Bhagavata Purana's treatment of power, responsibility, and the recognition of change?
