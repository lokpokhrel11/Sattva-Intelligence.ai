Bhagavata Purana · Episode 10

Purusha, Prakriti, and Parikshit's Search for Krishna

Canto 1, Chapters 10–12

Watch Episode 10 Return to Episode Index

Episode Overview

Episode 10 follows the narrative after Bhishma's death and Yudhishthira's return to rule. Krishna prepares to leave Hastinapura, the people watch his departure, and the narrative follows his journey toward Dvaraka. What is departure from one city becomes return for another.

Within this movement, Bhagavata Purana 1.10.21–22 introduces philosophically significant language concerning Purusha, Prakriti, the gunas, name, and form. The episode places this vocabulary alongside classical Samkhya while preserving the Bhagavata's own textual and theological setting.

The episode then turns to Parikshit. The child who had encountered Krishna before birth is born into the restored Kuru line. His name is connected with a continuing act of examination or searching: he looks among people for the figure he had previously seen.

Text Covered

Bhagavata Purana, Canto 1, Chapters 10–12.

Chapter 10 follows Krishna's departure from Hastinapura. Chapter 11 describes his return to Dvaraka and his reception there. Chapter 12 turns to the birth, naming, and predicted future of Parikshit and concludes with Yudhishthira's sacrifices and the restoration of royal life after the war.

Primary Source

The principal English source for this episode is G. V. Tagare's translation of the Bhagavata Purana. Chapters 10–12 are not included in Edwin F. Bryant's anthology used elsewhere in this series.

Sanskrit is consulted where wording directly affects interpretation, especially Bhagavata Purana 1.10.21, 1.10.22, and 1.12.30.

Key Textual and Philosophical Notes

1. Purusha in Bhagavata Purana 1.10.21

Bhagavata Purana 1.10.21 describes Krishna using the expression Purushah Puratanah, the ancient or primordial Purusha. The verse occurs as Krishna is leaving Hastinapura.

The episode pauses over this language because Purusha is also a major technical term in classical Samkhya. The shared vocabulary makes comparison useful, but it does not establish conceptual identity. The Bhagavata verse must first be read within its own narrative and theological setting.

2. Prakriti, Name, and Form in 1.10.22

The following verse brings Prakriti into the discussion and connects manifestation with Krishna's power. The episode also considers the language of name and form, nama and rupa, as the undifferentiated becomes manifest in differentiated existence.

This provides the immediate textual basis for comparing the passage with classical Samkhya's account of Purusha, Prakriti, and the gunas. The comparison is used to clarify differences as well as similarities.

3. The Samkhya Comparison

In classical Samkhya, Purusha and Prakriti are distinct principles. Purusha is consciousness, while Prakriti is the source of material manifestation and operates through the three gunas: sattva, rajas, and tamas.

The Bhagavata uses overlapping philosophical vocabulary, but the same Sanskrit terms do not necessarily perform identical technical functions in every text. In this passage, Krishna is not simply being inserted into classical Samkhya's dualistic structure.

The comparison therefore serves as a conceptual aid rather than a claim that the Bhagavata and classical Samkhya are presenting the same metaphysical system.

4. The Gunas Are Not Merely Personality Types

Sattva, rajas, and tamas can be discussed in relation to human psychology, as earlier episodes of this series have done. Their philosophical range is wider than personality classification, however. In Samkhya they belong to Prakriti itself and participate in the account of manifestation.

Episode 10 therefore uses the gunas at a cosmological and metaphysical level while preserving the psychological applications developed elsewhere in the series.

5. Departure and Return

Krishna's journey is described from two different relational perspectives. For Hastinapura, his movement is departure. For Dvaraka, the same movement is return.

Chapter 11 develops the second perspective through the city's reception of Krishna, including the sounding of his conch and the imagery surrounding his arrival.

6. Parikshit's Prenatal Encounter

Chapter 12 returns to the child whose life had been threatened before birth. Within the Bhagavata's narrative, Parikshit had already encountered the figure who protected him while he was still in the womb.

His later life therefore begins with a prior encounter that he cannot simply reproduce at will. The narrative connects seeing with a later search for recognition.

7. Why He Is Called Parikshit — 1.12.30

Bhagavata Purana 1.12.30 provides the textual basis for connecting Parikshit's name with examination or searching. Having previously seen the protecting figure, the child looks among people for the one he had seen before.

This connection is important for the episode title. Parikshit's search for Krishna is not added to the narrative from outside the text; it is grounded in the account associated with his naming.

8. The Future Listener

The predictions surrounding Parikshit's future extend beyond his childhood. The audience already knows that this child will eventually become the king who, facing death, listens to Shuka's teaching of the Bhagavata Purana.

The narrative therefore links the child's beginning with the listening situation from which much of the Bhagavata itself will unfold.

9. Yudhishthira, Marutta, and the Ashvamedha

The end of Chapter 12 returns to Yudhishthira. The postwar kingdom faces material consequences, including a depleted treasury. The narrative introduces wealth associated with King Marutta and describes Yudhishthira's performance of Ashvamedha sacrifices.

This closing material belongs to the episode's larger movement from destruction toward restored political and social order. Parikshit grows within that reconstructed world.

Important Terminological Caution

This episode uses comparison across textual traditions, especially between the Bhagavata Purana and classical Samkhya. Such comparison does not imply that a shared Sanskrit term has one fixed philosophical meaning across all Indic texts.

Purusha, Prakriti, and the gunas should therefore be interpreted first within the passage in which they occur. Cross-textual comparison can clarify a term, but it should not replace the conceptual framework of the source text.

Key Sanskrit Terms

Purusha (पुरुष) — a philosophically significant term whose meaning depends upon textual context. In classical Samkhya it denotes consciousness distinct from Prakriti; Bhagavata Purana 1.10.21 uses the term in reference to Krishna.

Prakriti (प्रकृति) — nature or the principle associated with manifestation; a central technical category in Samkhya and also used within the Bhagavata's own theological framework.

Guna (गुण) — the three gunas are sattva, rajas, and tamas. Their range extends beyond psychological personality categories.

Nama (नाम) — name.

Rupa (रूप) — form.

Parikshit (परीक्षित्) — the name of the child who later becomes king and the listener to Shuka's teaching of the Bhagavata. Chapter 12 connects the name with his examining or searching among people for the figure previously seen.

Ashvamedha (अश्वमेध) — the horse sacrifice associated in Chapter 12 with Yudhishthira's restored kingship.

Connections Across the Series

Episode 10 continues several themes developed in earlier episodes. The gunas connect back especially to Episode 2, where sattva, rajas, and tamas were examined in relation to perception and action.

Purusha and Prakriti also reconnect with the cosmological discussions of Episodes 3–5, while Parikshit's birth returns the series to the king whose final seven days framed Episodes 1 and 2.

The episode therefore joins two scales of the Bhagavata narrative: questions about manifestation and metaphysics on one side, and the life of a particular child on the other.

Discussion Question

Parikshit's life begins with a search for the figure he had encountered before birth, while the story of his final days will become a search through listening and inquiry. What does the Bhagavata gain by placing these two forms of searching at opposite ends of his life?
